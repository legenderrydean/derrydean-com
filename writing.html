Writing

Long-form essays, written from inside the work.

I write from the position of someone who built before the rules existed, then helped write the rules. No em dashes, no borrowed frameworks, and always a named first-person stake in the argument.

Most of what appears below starts as a longhand note before it becomes an argument, then a draft, then something I am willing to put my name to.

Long-form essay
~3,800 words

The $384.7 Billion Architecture

How Ghana built a digital payments market

"In 2008, before Ghana had anything resembling a functional digital payments market, we sold scratch cards. […] We manufactured that bridge ourselves, because there was no other way through the gap."

Written from inside DreamOval's founding and Derrydean's later chairmanship of the Ghana Chamber of Technology, this essay traces the fifteen-year regulatory sequence, from the 2008 Branchless Banking Guidelines through the 2019 Payment Systems and Services Act, that turned Ghana into a market processing an estimated $384.7 billion a year. It introduces two coined concepts: regulatory market-creation, the idea that a regulator can constitute a market before any actor can capture it, and the permission tax, the portion of a founding team's time consumed by institutional navigation rather than product-building. Comparative cases from Kenya, South Africa, and Nigeria show what happens when the sequence goes differently.

Policy essay
~2,900 words

No Economy Needs More Hours. Every Economy Needs More Roles.

Why developing economies consistently misdiagnose the employment problem

"Every government with a jobs problem eventually reaches for the same instrument: more activity extended across more time."

A structural critique of Ghana's 24-Hour Economy and Accelerated Export Development Programme, and of the broader policy reflex it represents. Argues that hour-extension policies consistently miss an architectural layer beneath the premise, and proposes what that layer actually requires.

Policy essay
~2,400 words

Strategy Before Rules

A new playbook for governing emerging technology

"The world's most advanced economies are writing rules for technologies they are still learning to understand. Developing economies are copying those rules before they have built the institutional infrastructure those rules were designed to protect."

A seven-pillar framework for governments regulating AI and emerging technology from a position of strategy rather than fear, replacing the reflex to borrow rules with the discipline to build institutional capacity first.
